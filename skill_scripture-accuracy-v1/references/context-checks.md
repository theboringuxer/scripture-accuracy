# Context checks

A short checklist for deciding whether a verse is being used responsibly. Work through it for any verse that carries weight in the piece (a headline verse, a key claim, a verse offered as a promise). Skip it for passing mentions.

## Contents
- Literary context
- Historical context
- Theological context
- Application vs. claim
- Common stretches
- How to write a context note

## Literary context

- **Read the surrounding verses.** Read at least the paragraph before and after. Many verses change meaning when the sentence they belong to is restored.
- **Identify the genre.** Narrative describes, it does not always prescribe. Proverbs state general patterns, not guarantees. Psalms voice prayer and emotion, including complaint. Epistles answer specific situations. Prophecy often has a near and a far horizon.
- **Identify the speaker and the audience.** Who is talking, and to whom? Words spoken to one person, one church, or one nation need care before they are applied to everyone.
- **Check the argument.** In the epistles, a verse is usually a step in a longer argument. Note what the author is arguing toward.

## Historical context

- **Original audience and situation.** Exiles, a persecuted church, a divided congregation, a king in crisis.
- **Covenant setting.** Is the text addressed to Israel under the old covenant, or to the church? That does not make it irrelevant. It changes how it is applied.
- **Cultural setting.** Note when a word or practice meant something different then than it does now.

## Theological context

- **Canon.** Does the use fit what the rest of Scripture says on the topic? A verse should not be used to contradict a clear teaching elsewhere.
- **Christ.** Many passages point to Christ. Note when the use skips over that.
- **Disputed readings.** Where traditions read a passage differently, say so briefly and do not pick a side. Suggest the user's pastor or a trusted commentary.

## Application vs. claim

Applying a verse is normal and good. The test is whether the writer says "this verse means X" when X is really an application. Fine: "Paul wrote this to a church in conflict, and the principle applies at work." Not fine: "This verse promises your project will succeed."

## Common stretches

| Verse | What it is often used for | What the context says |
|---|---|---|
| Jeremiah 29:11 | A personal promise of success and a good plan | Written to exiles in Babylon, within a promise of a long wait before restoration |
| Philippians 4:13 | Strength to win or achieve | Paul on contentment in plenty and in need |
| Romans 8:28 | Everything will turn out well | The promise is for those who love God and are called, and it is about God's purposes |
| Psalm 46:10 | Be quiet and calm | "Be still" in the middle of war, as a call to know that God is God |
| Matthew 18:20 | Any gathering of two or three | The passage is about church discipline |
| Proverbs (general) | Guarantees | General patterns of wisdom, not promises |
| Matthew 7:1 | Never judge anyone | Warning against hypocritical judging, followed by instruction to evaluate carefully |

Sayings that are **not in the Bible**: "God helps those who help themselves," "cleanliness is next to godliness," "God won't give you more than you can handle" (a distortion of 1 Corinthians 10:13, which is about temptation). Say so directly.

## How to write a context note

Keep it to one or two sentences: who the text was written to, what it was about, and how the piece is using it. Example:

> Paul wrote this from prison, about learning to be content in every circumstance. Using it here for perseverance in a hard job search is a fair application, but it is not a promise of a particular result.

If the use is reasonable, say so and move on. A context flag is a judgment call for the writer, not a ruling.
